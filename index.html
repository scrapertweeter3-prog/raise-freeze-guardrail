Run the raise before you hand it to the market

The common rule says inflation-proof every withdrawal every year, and the cheapest way to do that is the guardrail version: take the raise only if last year's balance finished above where it started. On the textbook plan, a $1,000,000 portfolio, 7 percent nominal return, $40,000 the first year, raises of 2 percent every year, the rule never fires. Thirty years of raises compound to a $71,034 withdrawal by year 30 and the balance finishes at $2,971,540, which is nearly triple the start.

Count what the plan actually spends. The first decade costs $437,987. The last decade costs $650,828, and that is 40.1 percent of the entire 30-year budget of $1,622,721. More than two fifths of the money changes hands after year 20, and a portfolio that nearly triples by the end has room to pay it. The plan never dips. The raise freeze is a rule for a portfolio that hovers at break-even; the balance pattern the rule watches for is exactly where textbook 4 percent never goes.

The guardrail is worth running for one reason: it prices the raise. A rule that never fires is not proof the raise is free, it is the market paying for it. The check is one comparison a year. If a downturn ever does dip the balance below its starting line, the guardrail skips one raise, and the skipped raise comes back compounding next year anyway, 2 percent on a $71,034 withdrawal being $1,421. The plan costs what it costs. The freeze just names who pays for it.
